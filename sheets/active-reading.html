Guide · English as a second language

Read actively. Understand more. Speak better.

Passive reading means letting the words pass by. Active reading means working with the text: asking questions, noticing how English expresses ideas, and then using those expressions yourself. For a non-native speaker, it helps you understand more today and speak and write better later.

Passive reading

  • Eyes move, mind wanders
  • Translates word by word in your head
  • Looks up every unknown word
  • Highlights half the page
  • Finishes and remembers little
  • Never uses the new language

Active reading

  • Reads with a question in mind
  • Reads in phrases and ideas
  • Guesses from context first
  • Marks only what matters, with a code
  • Summarizes in your own words
  • Steals useful phrases and uses them
01

From reading to speaking

Reading well doesn't automatically make you speak well. You get there in four stages, and each one needs a small, deliberate action.

Stage 1

Understand

Follow the main idea and the argument, not every word.

Do: preview first, read with a question, and guess from context.
Stage 2

Notice

See how English says it: word partners, sentence patterns, linking words.

Do: mark 3–5 useful phrases in each text you read.
Stage 3

Remember

Keep the phrases long enough that they become yours.

Do: keep a phrasebook and review it with spaced repetition.
Stage 4

Use

Say and write the phrases in your own sentences.

Do: retell the text aloud, and write a short paragraph using the new phrases.

These stages follow well-known ideas from language-learning research. You learn from input you can mostly understand (Krashen). You learn what you consciously notice (Schmidt). And trying to speak or write shows you the gaps in what you know (Swain).

02

Before, during, after

A simple routine for any text: an article, a design doc, a book chapter. It's based on the classic SQ3R method (Survey, Question, Read, Recite, Review), adapted for reading in a second language.

Before

Preview

1–3 minutes
  1. Skim the structureTitle, headings, first and last paragraphs, bold words, images.
  2. Predict"I think this is about… and the author will probably argue…"
  3. Ask 1–3 questionsWrite them down. Now you read to answer them.
  4. Activate what you knowWhat do you already know about the topic? Known context makes new words easier.
During

Engage

Most of your time
  1. Read in chunks, not wordsMove your eyes phrase by phrase: "in order to / reduce the risk / of downtime".
  2. Guess, then continueUnknown word? Guess from context and keep reading. Check it later.
  3. Mark with a codeUse a few symbols (see section 03), not a highlighter on everything.
  4. Pause at each sectionAsk yourself: "What did this part say?" Answer in one sentence.
  5. Watch the linking wordsHowever, therefore, despite, as a result: they show the logic.
After

Process

5–10 minutes
  1. Summarize from memoryClose the text. Write or say the main idea in 1–3 sentences.
  2. Answer your questionsThe ones you wrote before reading. What's still unclear?
  3. Check your guessesLook up the words you marked. Were you right?
  4. Save 3–5 phrasesAdd them to your phrasebook with the original sentence.
  5. ReactDo you agree? What would you ask the author?
03

Mark the text with a code

Use a small set of symbols so every mark means something. Here is an example paragraph marked up.

Example · a paragraph from an engineering blog (written for this guide)

The team decided to roll out the new cache gradually★ rather than all at once. In hindsight, this turned out to be the right call: a subtle bug only surfaced~ under heavy load, and because only five percent of traffic was affected, they could roll back within minutes. That said, the slower rollout came at a cost[ ]: the migration took three weeks instead of one. ?

~ "surfaced": my guess from context is "appeared". Check later.
[ ] Steal: "came at a cost", "in hindsight", "that said".

04

Unknown words and useful phrases

Two skills: deciding which words deserve your time, and saving phrases (chunks) rather than isolated words. Fluent speakers don't build sentences word by word. They use ready-made chunks.

Should I look this word up?

1
Can I understand the sentence without it?Yes → skip it for now. No → go to 2.
2
Can I guess it from context, word parts or the next sentence?Yes → write your guess and continue. No → go to 3.
3
Does it appear more than once, or in a heading?Yes → look it up now. It matters. No → mark it and check it after reading.
4
After reading: is it common and useful for me?Yes → add it to your phrasebook. No → let it go.

The "my own sentence" column matters most. That's where reading becomes speaking. Review the phrasebook with spaced repetition, for example flashcards in Anki, with the phrase on one side and your sentence on the other.

05

Choose your reading depth

Not every text needs close reading. Choose the depth for your goal, and check your understanding afterwards.

Skim

Read quickly for the main idea. Headings, first sentences, conclusion.

"Is this worth reading closely?"

Scan

Look for one specific thing: a name, a number, a config flag.

"Where does it say the default timeout?"

Close read

Read slowly, with marks, questions and a summary afterwards. Use this for texts you'll learn from.

"What is the argument, and do I agree?"
One-sentence testCan you state the main idea in one sentence without looking?
Explain it simplyExplain it to a colleague (or a rubber duck) in simple words. If you get stuck, you've found the gap.
Why and howCan you say why the author believes it, not only what they say?
ParaphraseRewrite one key sentence in different words. Keep the meaning, change the form.
06

Turn reading into speaking and writing

Climb this ladder. Each step asks a little more of you. Start at the bottom, and add a new step when the current one feels easy.

1

Read aloud

Read one paragraph aloud slowly, in phrase groups. It trains rhythm, stress and pronunciation.
Pause at commas and linking words. Stress the key words: "It came at a cost."
2

Shadowing

Play audio (an audiobook, a talk with a transcript) and speak along a fraction of a second behind.
Conference talks with transcripts work well. Choose 1–2 minutes and repeat 3 times.
3

Retell

Close the text and retell it aloud in 60 seconds. Record yourself.
"The article is about… The main point is… One example they give is…"
4

Use 3 phrases

Write a short paragraph, on any topic, using three phrases from today's reading.
A Slack message, a PR description, or a short journal entry all count.
5

Respond

Give your opinion: agree, disagree, add an example from your own work.
"I partly agree. In my experience, gradual rollouts also…"
6

Discuss or teach

Talk about it with someone: a colleague, a study partner, or an AI voice chat.
Share an article with your team and explain in two sentences why it's worth reading.

Summarizing

  • The article is mainly about…
  • The author argues that…
  • The key point is that…
  • In short, …

Giving an opinion

  • What I found convincing was…
  • I'm not sure I agree, because…
  • That makes sense, but what about…?
  • It depends on…

Connecting to experience

  • This reminds me of when we…
  • In my experience, …
  • We ran into the same problem with…
  • This would apply to our case if…

Asking for clarity

  • What do you mean by…?
  • Could you give an example of…?
  • So, if I understand correctly, …
  • How does that work when…?
07

Balance easy and hard reading

You need two kinds of reading. Most people only do the hard kind, get tired, and stop.

Extensive reading

Read a lot of easy material, for fluency and enjoyment
Difficulty
Easy. You know almost every word.
Speed
Fast, with no dictionary.
Goal
Reading speed, automatic understanding, seeing words in many contexts.
Examples
Novels you enjoy, blogs, news, graded readers.
How often
Most days. Enjoying it matters more than working hard at it.

Intensive reading

Study a short, harder text in depth
Difficulty
A bit hard, with some new words and structures.
Speed
Slow. Mark it up and reread it.
Goal
Notice language, collect phrases, practice for speaking.
Examples
One article section, a design doc, a well-written postmortem.
How often
A few short sessions a week, using the full routine above.

How hard should the text be? Count unknown words.

0–2 / 100
2–5 / 100
5+ / 100
Comfortable: extensive readingGood for intensive studyToo hard for now: choose an easier text

Research on second-language reading (e.g. Hu & Nation, 2000; Laufer) suggests readers need to know roughly 95–98% of the words to understand a text well without help. Quick test: count the unknown words in a 100-word paragraph.

08

What to read, as an engineer

Mix work texts, where you already know the context, with general texts that build everyday and conversational English.

Easy start

Documentation you use

You know the concepts, so the English is easier to follow. Notice how good docs phrase instructions and warnings.

High value

Engineering blogs and postmortems

Clear storytelling: problem → cause → fix → lessons. Good language to reuse for incident reports and design docs.

Argument

Design docs and RFCs

Great for the language of trade-offs: "we considered…", "the downside is…", "we chose X because…".

Conversation

Essays and opinion pieces

They teach how to state and defend a view. Good for meetings and discussions.

Fluency

Fiction and memoirs

Everyday, emotional and descriptive English you don't meet in technical writing. Read for fun.

Listening bridge

Talks with transcripts

Read the transcript, then listen, then shadow. This connects written and spoken English.

09

Use AI as a reading coach

AI is a patient tutor that's always available. Use it to check and deepen your own work, not to replace it.

Understand a hard sentence
"Explain this sentence in simpler English, then explain its grammar: '…'"
Check your summary
"Here's the article and my 3-sentence summary. What did I miss or get wrong? Correct my English and explain each correction."
Quiz yourself
"Ask me 5 questions about this text, one at a time. Wait for my answer before the next one."
Learn a phrase deeply
"Give me 3 natural example sentences with 'that said', and tell me when it sounds too formal."
Improve your own sentence
"Is this natural English? 'In hindsight, we should add an index first.' Give the most natural version and explain the difference."
Practice speaking
"In voice mode, discuss this article with me. Push back on my opinions and correct only my biggest mistakes."
Pick the right level
"Rewrite this paragraph at an easier level. Keep the key phrases so I can compare the two versions."
Build flashcards
"From my phrasebook below, make Anki cards: phrase on the front, a gap-fill sentence on the back."
Rule: Try first, then ask AI. Summarize before asking for a summary. Guess the word before asking for its meaning. If AI does the thinking for you, you don't learn.
10

Common traps

These habits feel productive, but they slow your progress.

Translating word by word

You understand each word, but lose the meaning of the sentence.

Instead: read in chunks and think in ideas. Translate a whole sentence only when you're stuck.
Looking up every word

Constant interruptions break your flow and you lose the thread.

Instead: use the four questions in section 04. Look up words after reading.
Highlighting everything

It feels like work, but choosing what matters is the actual learning.

Instead: use the symbol code, with at most one or two ★ per section.
Rereading instead of recalling

Rereading feels familiar, and that feels like learning, but it isn't.

Instead: close the text and summarize from memory.
Only reading hard texts

It's exhausting, progress is slow, and you quit.

Instead: do mostly easy extensive reading, plus short intensive sessions.
Collecting without using

A list of 500 words you never use in speech.

Instead: collect fewer phrases, and use each one in your own sentence within 48 hours.

A 25-minute daily routine

Doing a little every day beats doing a lot once in a while. This is a suggested starting plan. Adjust the times to fit your life.

2 min

Preview

Skim the text, predict, and write one question.

12 min

Read and mark

Read a short text or one section. Use the symbol code.

3 min

Summarize

Write or say 1–3 sentences from memory.

5 min

Phrasebook

Save 3 phrases and write your own sentence for each.

3 min

Say it

Read one paragraph aloud, or retell the text in 60 seconds.
